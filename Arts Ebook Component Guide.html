Bolded lead-in

Opens a paragraph with a short phrase so skimming teachers can find the point. Keep it to two to five words.

Start with looking. Before students make anything, give them two minutes to notice what is in a painting. Ask what they see, not what they think it means.

Name the move. When a student blends colors or repeats a rhythm, say what they did. Naming a choice helps them make it again on purpose.

Keep materials simple. Torn paper, pencils, and a few clean sponges go a long way in a crowded room.

Use <b class="lead"> inside a paragraph. Use it on 2 to 4 paragraphs in a row, then switch back to plain text.

Callout boxes

Four types, each with a color and a fixed job. The colored edge and the heading both signal the type, so color is never the only cue.

Big idea

The arts are a way of knowing, not only a reward for finishing other work. Students learn to observe, interpret, and communicate through them.

Try it today

Play 30 seconds of music and have students draw the energy they hear using only lines. No pictures of objects.

Watch for

Teachers who model a "right way" to draw can quietly teach students that only some of them are artists. Show examples, then invite variation.

In the classroom

Ms. Reyes's third graders retold a fraction lesson as a three-frame comic. The retell showed her exactly where each group got stuck.

Pick the type by purpose: idea explains, try invites action, watch warns, moment tells a story. Keep each under 50 words and use no more than two per screen.

Accordion headers

Hides optional depth so the main reading path stays short. Good for extensions, grade-level notes, and background research.

Making visible thinking part of the art task gives students a second way to show what they know, which helps learners who struggle with writing.

For K-2, shorten the task to one material and one question. Model aloud, then let students work beside a partner.

Cardboard, scrap paper, found objects, and shadows all work. Ask families to donate clean recyclables.

Several panels can be open at once. Write headers as questions or short action phrases. Never hide required instructions inside an accordion.

Strategy card

The anchor of each chapter. Summarizes a single teaching strategy with the facts a teacher needs to decide if it fits their day.

See, Think, Wonder

  • Grades K-6
  • 15 minutes
  • No special materials
  • Visual arts
  • Responding

Students look closely at an artwork, then share what they see, what they think is happening, and what they wonder. It builds observation and evidence-based talk.

Blue chips describe logistics. Gold chips tie to arts areas or standards. Keep chips to six or fewer.

Tabs for grade bands

Shows the same strategy at different levels without making teachers scroll past content that does not apply to them. Arrow keys move between tabs.

Point to one part of the artwork at a time. Students answer aloud with sentence starters like "I see..."

Students record three observations on sticky notes and sort them into "what I see" and "what I think."

Students write a claim about the artwork and support it with two details they can point to.

Use for grade bands or subject areas only. Content in each tab should be about the same length.

Numbered steps

Reserved for true sequences, like a lesson routine. Numbers appear only when order matters.

  1. Show the image. Give students a full minute of silent looking.

  2. Ask what they see. Collect answers without judging them.

  3. Ask what they think. Request evidence from the image for each idea.

  4. Ask what they wonder. Record questions on the board to revisit.
